# A good season, but a tier below the first

Just finished the second season of *Cyberpunk: Edgerunners* and I'm left with a feeling of: "Okay, that was it...". I don't think it's a bad season, but it feels like a significant downgrade from the first, both in terms of writing and animation. And, thinking about it... even the soundtrack.

The first season managed to build a really strong identity, combining dynamic animation, an aesthetic faithful to the *Cyberpunk 2077* universe, engaging characters, a solid script, and a memorable soundtrack. The second tried to go a different way, but I get the impression that some of the creative decisions ended up nerfing the very elements that made the original so special.

## The change in art direction and its potential impact on the animation

Starting with the animation, I believe the decision to radically change the art direction was one of the production's main issues.

From what I've researched, Bartosz Sztybor, a showrunner and producer linked to CD Projekt RED, established that the second season should have a different identity from the first. His initial proposal was to pay homage to 90s cinema, citing films like *Pulp Fiction* and *Léon: The Professional*. Riding this wave, the new director, Kai Ikarashi, decided to expand this inspiration, also incorporating the aesthetic of 80s and 90s anime.



![Roman and Talia](../cyberpunk-edgerunners-2/imagens/cabelo-anos-90-01.png)



This info left me particularly curious about the creative process. Was Sztybor mainly intending to adopt a narrative language inspired by the cinema of that era, while Ikarashi decided to take the proposal into the visual style as well? Interviews indicate there was an intention to visually differentiate the seasons, but they don't make it completely clear how far the initial proposal went or which decisions came later.

Anyway, I don't think the drawings are bad. The art is very well done, and I recognize the talent involved in building that aesthetic. However, the line art inspired by old anime simply isn't my personal taste.



![Talia](../cyberpunk-edgerunners-2/imagens/cabelo-anos-90-02.png)



Since aesthetics are largely a matter of taste, my most serious critique is with the animation itself. The second season features many hallmarks of limited animation:

* More static frames.
* Many shots that deliberately hide the characters' mouths during dialogue, skipping the need to animate lip movements.
* Combat sequences that feel less fluid than the first season.

At several points, I felt like I was watching a succession of elaborate illustrations with little movement between them, something I saw quite a bit in the TV version of *JJK2*. This particularly bothered me during action scenes, which was exactly one of the aspects where Studio Trigger had stood out so much in the first season.

With all these pieces, I have my theory about what might have happened behind the scenes. Nothing confirmed, just my own headcanon.

A significant change in art direction requires adaptations in almost every stage of production, since new patterns for drawing, composition, lighting, and visual effects can demand adjustments in workflows, revisions, and even rework. Depending on the available schedule, this can reduce the time allocated to animating certain sequences.

I wonder if this change ended up compromising the production planning, forcing the team to resort to animation-saving shortcuts to deliver the episodes.

It's also possible that some of these techniques were used deliberately as a stylistic choice, especially since they are also present in the first season, but they seem to have been better integrated there.

Personally, in the case of the second season, I doubt they were all purely artistic choices. In the end, the main issue is the result: regardless of the intention, I feel the animation lost fluidity, dynamism, and impact compared to the first season.

Personally, I would have preferred if they kept the original art style and focused their efforts on ensuring animation as fluid and well-executed as the first season.

## The soundtrack: one of the most forgettable elements of the season

Another aspect that disappointed me was the soundtrack.

Music was one of the most striking elements of the first season of *Edgerunners*. It didn't just serve as background music, it helped build the atmosphere, the identity, and, most importantly, the emotional impact of the story.

Some songs became so tied to certain events that hearing them again is enough to bring back the emotions of those scenes. Not by chance, several of them ended up being used in internet memes.

*I Really Want to Stay at Your House* is probably the most obvious example of this. Its association with David and Lucy turned the song into something practically inseparable from the story.



<YouTube id="KvMY1uzSC1E" titulo="Cyberpunk: Edgerunners | “I Really Want to Stay At Your House” by Rosa Walton | Music Video" />



In the second season, I felt absolutely nothing like that. The soundtrack seemed so insignificant to me that, when I finished the ten episodes, I realized I couldn't remember a single song.

No composition particularly caught my attention, none were associated with an important moment, and none made me want to go look them up to listen again.

This is especially disappointing in a work set in the *Cyberpunk 2077* universe, where music plays such an important role in building the atmosphere and cultural identity.

The first season managed to turn certain songs into part of the viewer's emotional experience. The second one just passed me by without leaving any relevant musical memory.

## The inconsistency of powers and the exaggerated spectacle

Another issue is how the second season handles character powers and tech mods. At certain points, the series seems to ditch the universe's own tech logic just to chase an increasingly exaggerated visual spectacle.

*Cyberpunk 2077* features extremely advanced tech, radical body mods, and individuals capable of superhuman feats. However, there is a technological logic behind these abilities, even when they are pushed to the limit for the sake of action.

In the second season, some power displays seem to break these limits without the script providing a convincing foundation for this scaling.

And this becomes especially obvious with Talia's transformations. Her abilities reach increasingly absurd proportions, with metal components floating around her, seemingly pulled by some kind of magnetism, until she turns into that giant mechanical creature, which looks more like a battle anime monster than something belonging to the *Cyberpunk 2077* universe.

It's not that the series provides zero technological explanation for these abilities, but it feels insufficient compared to what's happening on screen. The impression is that the powers just scale up simply because the story needs a bigger spectacle.

And it's not like the first season avoided exaggerations. David himself even uses an absurdly powerful cyber-skeleton in season 1. The difference is that the tech had a more convincing foundation within the narrative, with clearly established limitations and physical consequences. Even with something that extreme, you could still recognize a logic compatible with that universe.

In the second season, the scaling feels increasingly fantasy-based. Talia basically turns into a giant metal monster, D also ends up resorting to an extraordinary armor, and everything takes on proportions that, to me, feel more like a superpower anime than the sci-fi of *Cyberpunk*.

To make matters worse, even the visual execution failed to convince me.



![talia's final transformation](../cyberpunk-edgerunners-2/imagens/talia-final-transformation.png)



Aside from not particularly liking the design of Talia's final transformation, I think the sequence itself was poorly animated. The creature's movements didn't convey the weight, fluidity, or grandiosity I expected from a confrontation of that scale. At times, it looked almost like a static image with a moving background.

Instead of being impressed by the transformation, I ended up noticing the scene's visual limitations.

The result is that a sequence seemingly designed to be one of the season's big spectacles ended up being, in my eyes, one of the clearest examples of its issues with art direction and animation.

In the end, it doesn't bother me that *Cyberpunk* features absurd tech or extremely powerful characters. What bothers me is when the exaggeration feels like it exists for its own sake, without any build-up to make that spectacle convincing.

## The script and the struggle to create emotional connections

Maybe my biggest issue with the second season is the script, especially the difficulty I had in forming emotional connections with the characters.

In the first season, David, Lucy, Maine, Rebecca, and the others formed a group whose relationships were built through living together, conflicts, and shared experiences. You didn't need deep character development for us to care about them. We followed David's journey and, through it, ended up getting invested in the people around him.



![first season group](../cyberpunk-edgerunners-2/imagens/first-season-group.png)



In the second season, I missed that connection. The friendship between D and Roman, for example, never felt strong enough to justify so much loyalty. In several situations, I wondered why Roman didn't just abandon him. Maybe his fascination with the story he was recording was part of the answer, but I didn't always feel like the script sufficiently explored his motivations.

That's when I started questioning my own critique: **should these characters even be a team?**

Thinking about it, maybe I was expecting something from the second season that it never intended to offer. Unlike the first, here we follow individuals with their own stories whose paths eventually cross.

The problem, therefore, might not have been the lack of a team, but the way these stories were developed and connected.

Interestingly, some relationships worked very well for me. I really liked the youthful romantic tension between Talia and Roman, but especially her conflicts with her mother. There was a very strong emotional weight in watching Avida trying to save her daughter at any cost, while suffering as she realized that, with every new modification, that girl who once held her finger so tightly seemed to be getting lost a little more. It was the conflict of a mother who wanted to see her daughter cured, but feared that, in the process, she would stop being who she was.

Actually, I often found it more interesting to follow Talia, her relationship with Roman, and the mysteries of her past than to find out what would happen to the other protagonists.

And while writing this review, I realized something curious: **Talia was the common thread for almost everything that actually engaged me in the season.**



![Talia wave](../cyberpunk-edgerunners-2/imagens/talia-wave.png)



Her family conflicts, her rebellion, her insecurities, and the consequences of her body mods gave her relationships a human dimension that I felt was missing in the other stories.

D's revenge arc, the future's budget Sasuke, for example, never really won me over. There was mystery, violence, and action sequences, but his story felt distant, and honestly, a bit cliché. Weak also had an interesting premise: an old edgerunner forced to live with his own decay. However, I wish this conflict had received more attention, because I often felt like his role boiled down to just protecting Talia.

Roman, on the other hand, annoyed me in a different way.

His premise was one of the most interesting: a kid obsessed with movies, who rejected braindances and wanted to record real stories. However, as the season progressed, I got the impression that his own journey was losing importance. He followed D, recorded the events, and reacted to what was happening around him, but he rarely seemed to actually drive the story.

And I'm not talking about a lack of evolution here, but a **lack of agency**.

Roman didn't need to abandon his ideals or go through some massive transformation. A character can stay true to their convictions and still make decisions that significantly impact the events of the story.

His filming has consequences for the narrative, sure, but that's exactly where another problem arises: those consequences don't always feel like they stem naturally from his choices. I often felt like the script had to force importance onto whatever Roman was recording just to justify his presence in the story, instead of building that importance in a convincing way.

Researching Roman, I found out that, according to Bartosz Sztybor, the character emerged during development as a representation of the creators' own love for cinema.

This made me wonder if that artistic intention ended up overriding the needs of the narrative.

It doesn't bother me that Roman represents a personal passion of the authors. The problem is that, in my view, this idea wasn't integrated into the story organically enough. Even when his filming leads to important events, I often feel like they only become relevant because the script needs them to be, rather than because that importance was built throughout his journey.

It's as if the creators knew exactly what they wanted Roman to represent, but couldn't turn that representation into a character that was as dramatically interesting as his premise promised.

And maybe that's exactly why his symbolic function feels more obvious than his actual relevance within the story itself.

Thinking about that, another question occurred to me: **was the problem the absence of a clearly established protagonist?**

In the first season, David functioned as the narrative and emotional center. His decisions drove the story, and his relationships gave meaning to the events. Even when we disagreed with his choices, we understood his motivations and felt the weight of the consequences.

In the second, those roles seem split between different characters. D moves a good chunk of the plot, Weak has his own conflicts, and Talia carries most of the emotional weight. Roman... "I'm just happy to be here."

What bothered me was that these arcs, both individually and when they crossed paths, couldn't always spark the interest their premises promised. While D was driving the action, it was Talia who actually made me care about the characters' fates.

And I think I've finally identified what was missing from the script.

The first season managed to make me care about David's story and, consequently, about the people in it. The second season threw several stories at me, but few managed to hook me in the same way.

In the end, it didn't need another David, or another crew of mercenaries. It just needed those journeys, no matter how different they were, to turn their encounters, conflicts, and choices into something that actually made me care about what would happen next.

## A season that tried to do too much at once

Looking at the big picture, I get the impression that *Edgerunners 2* tried to pull off too many ambitions at once.

The production sought to establish a new artistic identity, pay homage to different cinematic traditions, work with multiple protagonists, develop parallel stories, and deliver increasingly massive confrontations. All of this in just ten episodes.

The result was a season with good ideas but uneven execution. The art direction changed radically, and the animation lost some of the fluidity and impact of the first season. The narrative adopted a more fragmented structure, but it didn't always manage to develop its stories and characters convincingly. The confrontations grew to massive proportions, sometimes drifting away from the technological logic of *Cyberpunk 2077*, while the soundtrack lost much of the emotional presence that had defined the series.

Individually, these choices would be debatable. Together, they left me feeling like the production was so worried about building something different that it ended up compromising part of what made *Edgerunners* special.

And maybe that is my biggest frustration with the second season.

The first season had found a very successful balance between visual identity, dynamic animation, captivating characters, an emotionally engaging narrative, and a memorable soundtrack. The second decided to overhaul much of that identity, and I'm still questioning the need for so many changes.

Would this drive to do something different stem from a genuine artistic need or, at least in part, from a certain intellectual vanity on the part of the creators, who were more interested in expressing their own ambitions than in evaluating if they would actually work within the piece?

Maybe the clearest example of this doubt is Roman. The idea of paying homage to cinema through a character is interesting, but if that homage cannot integrate naturally into the narrative, to what extent is it serving the story, rather than just the writer's ego?

Of course, I can't claim to know the creators' personal motivations, but the impression left is that some decisions were given more attention for what they intended to represent than for how they actually functioned on screen.

After all, innovating doesn't necessarily mean abandoning what works. A new story, with different characters and its own approach, could have expanded the *Edgerunners* universe without giving up so many of the elements that made the first season such a striking experience.



![sadge D](../cyberpunk-edgerunners-2/imagens/sadge-d.png)



**In the end, the second season didn't need to repeat the first. But it also didn't need to abandon so many of the elements that made it a success.**

## **Conclusion:**

*Cyberpunk: Edgerunners 2* is a good season. It remains a fun experience, set in a fascinating universe, and has its moments of action and emotion. However, due to the issues I discussed throughout this review, I consider it to have fallen considerably short of the first one.

It's an anime that entertains, but it's unlikely to leave such a lasting impression. It lacked that level of engagement that makes you care deeply about the characters, get anxious for the next episode, and keep thinking about the story long after you've finished it.

I don't consider it a bad season, much less a waste of time. It's a perfectly valid option for those who like *Cyberpunk* and are looking for something to watch and unwind. **But it's far from a must-watch experience. It's good while it lasts, it's just not particularly memorable.**
